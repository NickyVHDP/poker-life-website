Strategy & mindset

Poker Articles

Short pieces on the parts of the game that decide results over a long enough sample: what you pay attention to, what you are willing to fold, and how you judge whether you are actually playing well.

Part one

Focus

Attention is the cheapest edge available and the first one most players give away.

Article

The Information You Already Have

Before you look for a tell, use the data the table hands you for free: bet sizes, how long a decision took, which streets a player is willing to fire on, and who folds to a second barrel.

None of this requires reading a face. It requires being present for hands you are not in — which is precisely when most players check their phone.

Try this

Pick one opponent per session and track only their preflop raise sizes. One variable, watched properly, beats five watched vaguely.

Article

Hour Six

Your decisions in the first hour are rarely the problem. The leak lives late in a session, when fatigue quietly lowers the standard of every choice you make and nothing warns you it has happened.

Concentration is a finite resource. Treating it as unlimited is the same mistake as treating a bankroll as unlimited — it just takes longer to notice.

Try this

Decide your stop condition before you sit down, and make it about your attention rather than your stack.

Article

Playing the Player, Not the Story

It is tempting to build a narrative about an opponent from one memorable hand. Narratives are sticky, and a single bluff you caught can colour every read you make for the next two hours.

Focus means holding your reads loosely and updating them on evidence, not on the hand that annoyed you most.

Try this

After a hand that surprised you, ask what frequency it implies — not what kind of player it proves.

Part two

Patience

Folding never feels like progress. That is exactly why it is undervalued.

Article

Boredom Is a Bet Sizing Problem

Most loose calls are not strategic errors. They are entertainment purchases made by a player who has been card-dead for forty minutes and wants something to happen.

Recognising the impulse for what it is does more for a win rate than another chart. The hand you were about to play does not become good because the last twelve were bad.

Try this

When you feel the urge to enter a pot, name the reason out loud in your head. If the reason is “I haven’t played a hand in a while,” you have your answer.

Article

Waiting Is Not Passive

Patience is often mistaken for playing scared. The difference is what you do when the spot finally arrives: a patient player has folded their way into a tight image and then uses it, decisively.

Passivity folds and keeps folding. Patience folds in order to be believed later.

Try this

Track how often you actually convert a tight stretch into pressure. If the answer is rarely, you are being passive, not patient.

Article

The Hand You Did Not Play

You will never get feedback on a good fold. The pot gets pushed elsewhere, the cards go in the muck, and nothing confirms you were right. Meanwhile every bad call that happened to hit gets remembered vividly.

This asymmetry trains players in the wrong direction over years. Correcting for it is a deliberate act.

Try this

Note one disciplined fold per session in the same place you would note a big pot won. Give the good decisions equal shelf space.

Part three

Profit

A result you arrive at, not a plan you execute.

Article

Judge the Decision, Not the River

In a game with this much short-run variance, outcomes are a noisy signal about the quality of play. A correct call loses often enough to feel wrong, and a reckless one wins often enough to feel inspired.

If you grade yourself on results, the game will teach you the wrong lessons at random intervals. Grade the reasoning instead — it is the only part you control.

Try this

Review one hand you lost and one you won each session. Ask the same question of both: given what I knew, was that the best available choice?

Article

“I Just Ran Bad”

Sometimes that is true. Variance is real and it is brutal, and a good player can lose for a long stretch without doing anything wrong.

It is also the most comfortable sentence in poker, which is what makes it dangerous. It explains every losing session equally well, whether or not it is the actual explanation.

Try this

Before reaching for variance, find one decision in the session you would make differently. If you genuinely cannot, then it probably was variance.

Article

Game Selection Is Strategy

The largest single lever on your results is often not how you play a hand but which table you chose to play it at. Edge comes from the difference between you and the field, and you can change the field.

An average player in a soft game will outperform a strong player who insists on proving something in a hard one.

Try this

Spend five minutes choosing a seat before you spend five hours in it.

A note on expectations: nothing here is a guarantee of results. Poker involves real money and real short-term luck, and no article, book, or system removes that. These pieces are about improving the quality of your decisions — which is the only part of the game anyone can actually control.
